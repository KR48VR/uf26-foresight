Board two-pager

For the President and Board of Trustees

Crux

Farnam Jahanian is right that physical AI and autonomous science fit CMU’s history. The Robotics Innovation Center is open. The AI Science Foundry and three DOE Genesis awards are live. The hard problem is to turn that into a lasting franchise — rooms the country uses, and rooms a student can still use to start a field that does not yet have a sponsor — without becoming a contractor whose calendar is only other people’s statements of work.

Diagnosis in six lines

CMU’s comparative advantage is not “AI” as a slogan. It is SCS plus robotics plus engineering plus a campus that will build the thing. February 2026 opened the Robotics Innovation Center. NSF put $20 million into the AI Science Foundry. DOE’s Genesis Mission gave CMU three lead awards. Richard King Mellon Hall of Sciences is going up. The danger is the contractor temptation: take every national-priority dollar that looks like last decade’s robotics win, staff it with the same faculty, and wake up in 2032 as a superb execution shop that no longer sets the next field.

Guiding policy

Build the national infrastructure for AI-enabled physical work — robots, autonomous labs, scientific workflows — and keep a third of faculty time free to start fields that do not yet have a program office.

Coherent actions

  1. Treat the Foundry, Genesis and RIC as one franchise with a single operating system, not three press releases.
  2. Write a “new field” allocation: a fixed share of SCS and Engineering hiring that is not mapped to a current federal mission.
  3. Use Heinz as the public-trust arm, so CMU is not only a builder of capable systems.
  4. Keep undergraduate intensity as a constraint on growth — the product includes the culture of making, not only research volume.

Full report

What CMU is

A specialist research university that punches above its scale because it was willing to be a computer-science and robotics institution before that was an obvious luxury brand. Not a comprehensive giant. A place that builds. Jahanian’s 2026 State of the University said the quiet part: the national priorities now look like CMU’s existing strengths. That is a gift with a fuse.

Forces that actually move this institution

  • Physical AI as industrial policy. Robots, autonomous labs, and scientific agents are DOE/NSF/national-lab work.
  • Scale mismatch. Demand exceeds faculty size. Growth can dilute the culture that created the demand.
  • Coastal gravity. Faculty and PhDs can leave for labs and coastal chairs. Pittsburgh must win on the work.
  • Campus-building cycle. RIC is open. Hall of Sciences is rising. Capital projects can freeze an old org chart in steel.

Kernel Reconstruction, September 2026 — not the published plan

Diagnosis

CMU is being offered the role of America’s shop floor for AI-in-the-world. It should take the role. It should not take the role as its identity.

Guiding policy

Be the university that builds physical AI as public infrastructure and still behaves like a place that can invent the next SCS. Contractor dollars fund the platform. Investigator time funds the surprise.

Coherent actions

One franchise operating system across Foundry / Genesis / RIC. Protected investigator hiring share. Heinz as the trust-and-measurement arm. Undergraduate build-time as a hard constraint. Pittsburgh retention built on unique facilities, not matching coastal cash blindly.

Critical uncertainties

Axis A — Role in the national system. Trusted public infrastructure for physical AI, or a high-end contractor executing other people’s missions.

Axis B — Institutional character. The CS-and-making culture still starts new fields, or scale and mission money freeze today’s org chart.

Infrastructure · Field-starting

The Foundry University

2030

The Robotics Innovation Center and the autonomous labs are booked by people who do not go to CMU — national labs, firms, agencies — and by undergraduates who still get bench time. SCS has spun out at least one new concentration that did not exist on a 2026 slide. A first-year can still build something that is not a work package. Faculty who take mission money still have one afternoon a week that is not billed to it.

2040

CMU is the place you go to learn how intelligence acts in the physical world, and to start the field after that. A twenty-two-year-old can still propose a concentration. Employers come for robotics and stay for people who can open a new line. The national missions paid the light bill. They did not write the catalog.

Contractor · Field-starting

Moonlighting Shop

2030

Mission money dominates the weekday calendar: Genesis, RIC, the foundry. New fields happen at night and on weekends, when the best people still have the energy. Undergraduates who want that second project learn to find faculty after 7 p.m. It works. Barely. A year of bad hiring would kill it.

2040

A generation later, “night and weekend” has lost. The people who could start fields took jobs where that work is the day job. CMU is superb at the missions it already has. The next SCS — the thing that made this campus matter last time — happened somewhere with a less booked calendar.

Infrastructure · Frozen

National Workshop

2030

Execution is superb. Hiring maps to the 2026 missions: robotics, autonomous science, the DOE work. A candidate who wants to start something unnamed is a “poor fit.” Undergraduates get excellent training for the jobs those missions created. They do not see a faculty member invent a course from scratch.

2040

CMU is a respected national facility. Visitors fly in, use the rooms, fly out. The next School of Computer Science — the cultural fact, not the building — happens somewhere less booked. Alumni are proud. They hire from a younger campus when they want surprise.

Contractor · Frozen

Task Order U

2030

Revenue is fine. Identity is a set of contract vehicles. A department meeting is a review of statements of work. Students are staffed onto tasks in their second year. The phrase “Carnegie Mellon” on a paper means the work was done here. It does not mean the question was born here.

2040

Carnegie Mellon is a brand on other people’s statements of work. The rooms are excellent. The craft is real. The permission to start a field without a sponsor has to be sought somewhere else. Reunion tours walk past plaques for programs that ended when the task order ended.

Implications

The bad strategy is to say yes to every mission that resembles robotics. Across worlds, slack — hiring not mapped to today’s program office — is the strategy.

For students · CMU

If you wanted a spectator university, you came to the wrong shop floor.

CMU will try to hire your attention into existing national missions. That work is real. Leave room to start something that does not yet have a sponsor.

Best case

The Foundry University

You will build in rooms the country uses, and you can still invent a concentration if you find one faculty member who has an unbilled afternoon. That combination is why you came. Use the official facility. Guard the unofficial hour.

Squeeze

Moonlighting Shop

The official work will be missions. Your own work will happen after hours. Guard that time on a calendar someone else can see, or it will disappear. A portfolio that is only task-order output is a résumé, not an education.

Job shop

National Workshop

You will get excellent training for 2026 jobs. Add a question the job description does not contain — a system, a paper, a robot behavior nobody asked for — and put your name on it. Otherwise you are staff.

Worst case

Task Order U

You will be staffed onto someone else’s statement of work. Keep a portfolio that is yours: a repo, a paper, a demo that does not list a contract number. If everything you ship has a sponsor’s logo first, you already work there.

Do this while you are here

  • Ship one physical system — robot, instrument, lab workflow — not only a model card.
  • Take a Heinz or measurement-science course even if you are in SCS.
  • Work on one project that has no current program office.
  • Use RIC and the Foundry as a student, not as a tourist.
