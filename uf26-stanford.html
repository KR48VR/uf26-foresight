Board two-pager

For the President and Board of Trustees

Crux

Stanford sits next to the companies that now pay for more of its research than Washington does. Those companies also hire the people who would have been assistant professors. The hard problem is to stay a seven-school university that still chooses its own questions — Medicine, Law, Education and the humanities in the work, not only in the ethics appendix — before it becomes a well-branded annex of the labs down the road.

Diagnosis in six lines

Stanford invented much of the modern AI stack and still sits next to the firms that commercialized it. That adjacency used to be an advantage with a manageable cost. In 2026 the cost is the strategy. Federal sponsored research is roughly flat; nonfederal sponsored research is up nearly 50 percent and may exceed federal this year. Companies, not agencies, now drive most of the investment that sets the research agenda. HAI has been merged with Data Science under James Landay; Fei-Fei Li advises Levin university-wide; John Hennessy co-chairs the HAI council. AIMES and PACE are the undergraduate counters — classroom experiment plus intellectual rigor. None of that yet answers the presidential question: who chooses the problems, who keeps the people, and whether Medicine, Humanities, Law, Education, and Earth still sit at the same table as CS.

Guiding policy

Be the open, human-centered counterpart to closed industry labs — not their talent annex, and not a CS institute with a liberal-arts museum. Problem choice stays with faculty. Industry money is welcome when it buys instruments and fellowships, not when it buys the question.

Coherent actions

  1. Write an explicit problem-choice doctrine for industry AI gifts and affiliates: what Stanford will not let a sponsor specify.
  2. Use Fei-Fei Li’s presidential advisory role as an allocation device across all seven schools, not as a communications title — funded joint appointments in Medicine, Law, Education, and H&S, not more CS chairs.
  3. Make AIMES produce assessed classroom designs within two years, then retire “faculty freedom to experiment” as the whole undergraduate policy.
  4. Build a retention offer for AI-adjacent faculty that is not only money: compute, students, and permission to publish — the three things a lab can starve.
  5. Treat nonfederal exceeding federal as a structural shift, not a good year: a treasury and conflict-of-interest regime for a university whose largest research sponsor class is now private.

Full report

What Stanford is

A private research university on the San Francisco Peninsula whose founding bet was that a university next to industry could set industry’s questions rather than take them. Seven schools. A farm that became the capital of the American technology industry. John McCarthy’s Stanford AI Lab dates to 1963; HAI was the 2019 attempt to put humans back in the name of that tradition. Jonathan Levin, an economist, is the president who has to govern that tradition in a year when industry compute and industry salaries outrun the federal grant.

Forces that actually move this institution

  • Sponsor inversion. Levin told the Academic Council in 2026 that federal funding to faculty is roughly constant and nonfederal sponsored research is up nearly 50 percent — and may exceed federal this year. That is not diversification. It is a change in who sets the agenda.
  • Talent market next door. The same firms that fund research hire the people who would have been assistant professors and sixth-year PhDs. Adjacency now has a negative sign on the labor account.
  • Closed frontier. The most capable models and the largest training runs live in companies. Academic openness is a choice that now has to be financed, not a default of the field.
  • Seven-school test. Levin has said an era of abundant technical skill should be a golden age for the humanities at Stanford. That is a hope. The budget will decide it.
  • Assessment lag. Faculty have been left to experiment: some require AI, some have returned to oral exams and blue books. AIMES exists to turn that scatter into a university position.
“AI will evolve in ways we can’t predict, but the principles guiding our work — openness, excellence, human-centeredness — will be enduring.” — James Landay, on the merged HAI

Crux

Keep problem choice and a seven-school university when the Valley can pay more, compute more, and publish less — and when the easy strategy is to become the most elegant annex those labs have ever had.

Kernel Reconstruction, September 2026 — not the published plan

Diagnosis

Stanford’s historic advantage was proximity plus openness. Proximity now bids away people. Openness is no longer how the frontier is built. The 2026 reorganization treats a structural shift as an organizational-chart problem. Charts do not keep faculty, and they do not decide whether a medical school or a law school is in the AI story or only asked to write the ethics chapter.

Guiding policy

Stanford is the public square of frontier AI, not the farm team. Industry funds instruments and fellowships; faculty fund questions. Every school is a production site for AI-era knowledge, not a consumer of Engineering’s leftover tools. Undergraduate education must certify work an agent cannot submit.

Coherent actions

Problem-choice doctrine for affiliates and gifts, published, with examples of declined money. A seven-school AI appointment pool controlled from the president’s office. AIMES assessment compact by AY2028: each school names the assignments that remain human. Retention offers built around compute, advisees, and publication rights. A Board-level private-sponsor risk committee, because the largest research sponsor class is no longer the United States government.

Critical uncertainties

Axis A — Who owns the stack. Academic openness still sets norms and trains the field, versus industry labs capture models, data, and the best people, leaving Stanford to write papers about systems it cannot run.

Axis B — What Stanford is for. A seven-school university in which medicine, law, education, humanities, and Earth are co-authors of the AI era, versus a technology holding company with professional schools attached.

Open stack · Seven schools

The Open Agora

2030

A third-year in CS and a third-year in Medicine sit the same hospital clinic: the model drafts a differential, they have to say out loud what they accepted and what they overrode, and the note in the chart is theirs. HAI is where visiting labs come when they want a method they can cite, not a model they can license. Oral defenses are normal outside Engineering. Industry still funds instruments and fellowships. Faculty still pick the question. Other universities copy the clinic format because it produces people who can argue with a system in a room.

2040

Governments and firms still come to the Farm when they need an argument that is not a product roadmap. A law clinic, an education school trial, and a medical deployment can share a method without sharing a sponsor. The undergraduate degree still means you can defend work an agent drafted. Stanford did not become smaller, richer, and narrower. It remained a university next to industry rather than a campus inside one.

Captured stack · Seven schools

Ethics Wrapper

2030

Every school has an AI center and a well-designed impact statement. The models those statements describe were trained in Mountain View and Menlo Park. Nonfederal money pays the centers’ rent. A humanities seminar can critique a system it cannot run. A medical AI group validates tools it did not specify. Undergraduates are fluent in frameworks. Employers hire them to write the slide that explains someone else’s model.

2040

The seven schools still exist and still photograph well. They do not set the frontier. Stanford is the most prestigious ethics-and-translation layer on systems designed elsewhere. Graduates are good at the meeting after the technical meeting. The Farm’s comparative advantage is tone and access, not the next question.

Open stack · Tech holding

Beautiful Institute

2030

CS, EE and a sliver of Medicine are electric: open weights, real compute, packed labs. History, Education and much of H&S shrink with dignity — fewer graduate slots, slower hiring, a polite story about “focus.” AIMES becomes an engineering-education project. A non-technical major can still get a Stanford diploma. They cannot get Stanford’s new attention.

2040

Stanford is the world’s best private technical institute that still holds a football Saturday. Parents still buy the seven-school story. The budget does not. The founding claim — a full university next to industry — survives as a brochure sentence and a reunion toast.

Captured stack · Tech holding

The Annex

2030

Named labs sit on campus with Stanford letterhead and company roadmaps. A PhD is a four-year interview. Undergraduates pay for proximity: internships are easy, independent work is not. Levin’s human-centered language survives in speeches. Faculty who want to publish a negative result about a sponsor’s system do that work after they leave.

2040

The brand is durable. The land is valuable. The degree still opens doors in the Valley. The institution itself is a real-estate and credentialing wrapper around firms that no longer need a university to invent. Reunion tours walk past rooms whose research questions were chosen somewhere else.

Implications

A policy of “remain excellent and stay close to industry” is not a policy. It is how Stanford sleepwalks into The Annex. The bad strategy is to treat the HAI merger as the strategy, keep undergraduate AI as local faculty preference, and let private sponsors replace federal ones without a doctrine of refused gifts. The only policy that works across worlds is to defend problem choice and to put non-CS schools in production, not in commentary.

For students · Stanford

Proximity is not a major. It is a current. Swim, or get carried.

The lab that will try to hire you is closer than the library. That can make you serious. It can also make you a very expensive intern with a Cardinal hoodie. Use the seven schools while they still behave like one university.

Best case

The Open Agora

You will work with agents and still have to defend a claim in a room with the wifi off. You can take a Law, Medicine or Education studio that is building a real method — a clinic protocol, a school trial, a filing — not writing a warning label for someone else’s product.

Comfortable trap

Ethics Wrapper

You will become fluent in impact frameworks and stakeholder maps. The systems that matter are trained off campus. Employers will hire you to write the paragraph that makes a model sound responsible. That is a job. It is not the reason you came.

Narrow win

Beautiful Institute

If you are in CS or EE, the place is electric and the recruiters already know your name. If you are in History, Education or a quieter science, you will feel the budget before you feel the vision. Cross-register while the catalog still lets you.

Worst case

The Annex

Your degree will mainly certify that you lived near the industry. Build a body of work — a paper, a product, a clinic result — that would still matter if the return address were Urbana or Atlanta instead of Palo Alto.

Do this while you are here

  • Take one course that forbids agents and one that requires them. Learn the difference in your own work, not from a policy PDF.
  • Get a faculty relationship outside Engineering. That is the hedge if Stanford narrows.
  • Publish or ship something with your name on the question, not only on the implementation.
  • Treat internships as fieldwork on how problem choice actually happens in a lab. Write that down.
  • Use AIMES and PACE while they are still forming. Students who help design assessment will own the next catalog.
